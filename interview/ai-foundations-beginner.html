👋 Opening: about you

Almost every interview starts here. These answers set the tone, so prepare them word for word and practise them out loud.

  1. 1Tell me about yourself.

    💬 Easy answer

    Use present, past, future, in about 60 to 90 seconds. Present: "I'm a Java backend developer with four years at a fintech company, where I build payment APIs." Past: "Over the last year I moved towards AI. I built a document question answering app with RAG and an evaluation set, and I used an LLM to classify support tickets at work, which cut manual triage by about 30%." Future: "Now I want to do this full time as an AI engineer, and this role fits because you build LLM features on top of real backend systems, which is exactly where my strengths meet."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Present: current role and one concrete thing you do
    • Past: the 1 or 2 experiences that lead to this role, with a number
    • Future: why this role and this company is the logical next step
    • Keep it under 2 minutes; don't recite your whole resume

    What they're really checking: Can you tell a clear, relevant story about yourself? It's also a test of communication: structure and confidence matter more than detail.

    📖 Learn it: Landing the AI role: resume, applications and interviews

  2. 2Why do you want to move into AI, and why this particular role?

    💬 Easy answer

    Give a real reason and connect it to evidence. "In my testing work I kept seeing the same problem: thousands of test cases written and checked by hand. When I tried an LLM to draft test cases, it saved hours, but it also made mistakes I had to catch. That got me interested in building AI systems that are actually reliable. I've since completed projects in RAG and evaluation. I'm applying for this role because it focuses on shipping AI features to users, and my background in quality and testing directly helps with evaluating AI output."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • A specific moment or problem that sparked the interest (not "AI is the future")
    • Proof you've acted on it: courses finished, projects built
    • How your previous experience transfers to this role
    • Why this role, not just AI in general

    What they're really checking: Is your interest genuine and backed by action? Interviewers worry about people chasing hype who will lose interest.

    📖 Learn it: Landing the AI role: resume, applications and interviews · Building a portfolio project that gets interviews

  3. 3Why do you want to work here?

    💬 Easy answer

    Show you've done homework. Mention one thing about their product, one thing about how they work, and link both to you. "I've used your app for expense reports, and I noticed the receipt scanning recently got much better, so I read your engineering blog about it. I like that your team publishes how you evaluate models, because I care about measuring AI quality. And since you serve small businesses in India, my experience building for that market would help from day one."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Something specific about their product or users
    • Something about their team, values or engineering approach
    • Why you fit: a direct link to your skills
    • Avoid answers that fit any company ("great culture", "big brand")

    What they're really checking: Have you researched the company, and are you likely to stay?

    📖 Learn it: Landing the AI role: resume, applications and interviews

  4. 4You haven't had an AI job title yet. Why should we hire you over someone who has?

    💬 Easy answer

    Don't be defensive. Turn the gap into a strength. "That's fair. What I bring is production experience: I've spent five years shipping and supporting real systems, handling incidents and working with stakeholders. Many AI projects fail at exactly that stage, after the demo. On the AI side, I've built two end to end projects with evaluation, which I can show you. So you'd get someone who learns fast and already knows how to make software work in production."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Acknowledge it calmly in one sentence
    • Name the transferable strengths (production, domain, communication)
    • Show evidence of AI skills: projects, results, code
    • End with what you'll deliver, not what you lack

    What they're really checking: How do you handle a tough question about a weakness? Calm, honest and evidence based beats defensive.

🧱 Fundamentals

Short concept questions to check your basics. Answer in 3 to 5 sentences, with one example.

  1. 5What's the difference between AI, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI?

    💬 Easy answer

    They are circles inside each other. AI is the broad goal of making computers do things that seem intelligent. Machine learning is one way to do that: instead of writing rules, we let the computer learn patterns from examples. Deep learning is machine learning with many layered neural networks, which works well for images, audio and text. Generative AI uses deep learning models, like LLMs, to create new content such as text, images or code, instead of only predicting a label.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • AI ⊃ ML ⊃ deep learning; GenAI is an application of deep learning
    • ML learns from data instead of hand written rules
    • One example for each (spam filter, image recogniser, ChatGPT)

    What they're really checking: Can you explain basics clearly without jargon? This is often the very first technical question for freshers and PMs.

    📖 Learn it: What is AI? AI vs ML vs Deep Learning vs GenAI

  2. 6Explain supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning, with an example of each.

    💬 Easy answer

    Supervised learning learns from examples that have the right answer, called labels. For example, emails marked spam or not spam, so the model learns to predict spam. Unsupervised learning has no labels; it finds structure by itself, like grouping customers into segments by behaviour. Reinforcement learning learns by trial and error with rewards, like a game playing agent or a robot that gets a reward when it does the task well. LLMs also use reinforcement learning from human feedback to make answers more helpful.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Supervised = labelled data, predicts a known target
    • Unsupervised = no labels, finds groups or patterns
    • Reinforcement = actions, rewards, learning by trial and error
    • A real example for each

    What they're really checking: Basic vocabulary and whether you can give examples on the spot.

    📖 Learn it: How machines learn: features, labels and training · Two kinds of prediction: regression and classification

  3. 7What is overfitting, and how do you prevent it?

    💬 Easy answer

    Overfitting is when a model memorises the training data, including its noise, so it scores very well on training data but badly on new data. It's like a student who memorises last year's answers but can't solve a new question. You spot it when training accuracy is high and validation accuracy is much lower. To prevent it: get more data, use a simpler model, add regularisation, use cross validation, stop training early, and for neural networks use dropout.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Definition: good on training data, poor on unseen data
    • How to detect: compare train vs validation scores
    • At least three fixes: more data, simpler model, regularisation, early stopping, dropout
    • Mention underfitting as the opposite

    What they're really checking: The single most asked ML concept. They want the detection method, not only the definition.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  4. 8What is the bias variance trade off?

    💬 Easy answer

    Bias is error from a model that's too simple, so it misses real patterns. That's underfitting. Variance is error from a model that's too sensitive to the training data, so small changes in the data change it a lot. That's overfitting. Making a model more complex usually lowers bias but raises variance. The goal is the sweet spot where total error on new data is lowest, which we find using validation data. For example, a straight line through curved data has high bias; a very deep decision tree has high variance.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Bias = too simple, underfits
    • Variance = too sensitive, overfits
    • Complexity trades one for the other; pick by validation error
    • An example of each

    What they're really checking: Whether you understand why models fail, not just how to train them.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  5. 9Why do we split data into training, validation and test sets?

    💬 Easy answer

    The training set is what the model learns from. The validation set is used while building, to compare models and tune settings like depth or learning rate. The test set is kept locked away until the very end to get an honest estimate of how the model will do on new data. If you tune on the test set, you slowly overfit to it and your final number becomes too optimistic. For time based data, split by time, so you train on the past and test on the future.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Train = learn, validation = choose and tune, test = final honest check
    • Never tune on the test set
    • Split by time for time series; split by user or group to avoid leakage

    What they're really checking: Experiment discipline. Many candidates forget the role of the validation set.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  6. 10Explain precision and recall. When would you prefer one over the other?

    💬 Easy answer

    Precision: of everything the model flagged as positive, how many were really positive. Recall: of all the real positives, how many the model caught. For a cancer screening test or fraud detection, missing a real case is very costly, so we prefer high recall, even if we get some false alarms. For a spam filter, putting a real email into spam is annoying, so we prefer high precision. F1 score combines both when you need one number, and you can move between them by changing the decision threshold.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Precision = correctness of positive predictions
    • Recall = coverage of actual positives
    • Choose based on which mistake costs more, with an example
    • Threshold controls the trade off; F1 balances them

    What they're really checking: Whether you can connect a metric to a business cost.

    📖 Learn it: Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall

  7. 11Your model is 99% accurate, but the business says it's useless. How is that possible?

    💬 Easy answer

    Probably the classes are imbalanced. If only 1% of transactions are fraud, a model that always says "not fraud" is 99% accurate and catches zero fraud. Accuracy hides that. I'd look at the confusion matrix, precision and recall for the rare class, and metrics like PR AUC. Then I'd check the business goal: maybe they need to catch 80% of fraud while keeping false alarms low enough for the review team to handle, and I'd tune the threshold for that.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Class imbalance makes accuracy misleading
    • Use confusion matrix, precision, recall, PR AUC
    • Tie the metric to the business goal and capacity
    • Fixes: threshold tuning, class weights, resampling

    What they're really checking: Can you think beyond the headline number? Very common in 2026 interviews.

    📖 Learn it: Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall

  8. 12What is regularisation? What's the difference between L1 and L2?

    💬 Easy answer

    Regularisation adds a penalty for large weights to the loss, which keeps the model simpler and reduces overfitting. L2, also called ridge, penalises the square of weights, so it shrinks all weights smoothly towards zero but rarely to exactly zero. L1, also called lasso, penalises the absolute value, which pushes some weights to exactly zero, so it also does feature selection. In neural networks we also use dropout and weight decay, which is L2 in practice.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Penalty on weight size added to the loss
    • L2 shrinks smoothly; L1 makes some weights exactly zero
    • Strength is a hyperparameter tuned on validation data

    What they're really checking: Standard ML theory; they may follow up with why L1 creates zeros.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting · Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve

  9. 13What's the difference between a random forest and gradient boosting?

    💬 Easy answer

    Both combine many decision trees. A random forest trains lots of deep trees independently on random samples of rows and features, then averages their votes. That reduces variance and is hard to break. Gradient boosting trains small trees one after another, where each new tree fixes the errors of the ones before it. Boosting, like XGBoost or LightGBM, usually gives the best accuracy on tabular data but needs more careful tuning and can overfit if you add too many trees.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Forest = parallel trees, averaged, reduces variance
    • Boosting = sequential trees correcting errors, reduces bias
    • Boosting is often the strongest for tables; forests are robust defaults

    What they're really checking: Practical model knowledge for tabular data, which is still most industry ML.

    📖 Learn it: Decision trees, random forests and boosting

  10. 14Explain gradient descent in simple words. What does the learning rate do?

    💬 Easy answer

    Imagine you're on a hill in fog and want to reach the bottom. You feel which way the ground slopes and take a step downhill, again and again. Gradient descent does the same with the model's error: the gradient tells us which direction increases the error, so we move the weights a small step in the opposite direction. The learning rate is the step size. Too big and you overshoot and bounce around; too small and training is very slow. In practice we use mini batches and optimisers like Adam.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Loss = how wrong the model is; gradient = direction of steepest increase
    • Update weights opposite to the gradient
    • Learning rate = step size, with the risk of too big or too small
    • Mention mini batch SGD or Adam

    What they're really checking: Do you understand how models actually learn?

    📖 Learn it: Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve

  11. 15How does an LLM generate text?

    💬 Easy answer

    An LLM is a neural network, a transformer, trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token, which is a word or piece of a word. When you send a prompt, it's split into tokens, the model calculates probabilities for every possible next token, picks one, adds it to the text, and repeats until it's done. Settings like temperature control how random that choice is. Because it predicts plausible text rather than looking up facts, it can confidently produce wrong answers, which we call hallucinations.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Trained to predict the next token
    • Generates one token at a time in a loop
    • Temperature controls randomness
    • Explains why hallucinations happen

    What they're really checking: Basic GenAI literacy, asked for nearly every role now.

    📖 Learn it: What is an LLM? Tokens, context and temperature

  12. 16What do temperature and top p control? What would you use for a data extraction task?

    💬 Easy answer

    Both control randomness when choosing the next token. Temperature reshapes the probabilities: low temperature makes the model pick the most likely tokens, so it's more consistent; high temperature makes it more creative and varied. Top p limits choices to the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p. For extraction or classification I'd use temperature 0 or close to it, plus a fixed output format, because I want the same input to give the same, correct output. For brainstorming I'd raise it.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Both control randomness of token choice
    • Low = consistent, high = creative
    • Extraction: temperature near 0 and structured output

    What they're really checking: Practical prompting judgement.

    📖 Learn it: What is an LLM? Tokens, context and temperature · Prompting: giving clear instructions to an LLM

  13. 17What's the difference between a list, tuple, set and dictionary in Python? When would you use each?

    💬 Easy answer

    A list is an ordered, changeable collection; good for sequences you add to, like rows to process. A tuple is ordered but can't be changed; good for fixed records like coordinates, and it can be a dictionary key. A set holds unique items with very fast membership checks; good for removing duplicates or checking "have I seen this ID?". A dictionary maps keys to values with fast lookup; good for things like user ID to user details. Checking membership in a set or dict is O(1) on average, but O(n) in a list.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • List: ordered, mutable
    • Tuple: ordered, immutable, hashable
    • Set: unique, fast membership
    • Dict: key to value, fast lookup; mention O(1) vs O(n)

    What they're really checking: Python fluency, plus whether you think about performance.

    📖 Learn it: Lists and dictionaries: storing many values

  14. 18Explain INNER JOIN vs LEFT JOIN with an example.

    💬 Easy answer

    An INNER JOIN returns only rows that match in both tables. A LEFT JOIN returns every row from the left table, plus matches from the right; where there's no match, the right side columns are NULL. Example: customers and orders. An inner join gives only customers who have ordered. A left join from customers gives all customers, and those with NULL order IDs are the ones who never ordered, which is a common way to find inactive customers.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Inner = only matching rows
    • Left = all left rows, NULLs where no match
    • Use a left join plus IS NULL to find missing matches
    • Watch for duplicate rows when the join key isn't unique

    What they're really checking: The most common SQL basic. A mention of the duplicate row trap stands out.

    📖 Learn it: SQL JOINs: combining tables

  15. 19What are window functions? Explain ROW_NUMBER, RANK and DENSE_RANK.

    💬 Easy answer

    Window functions calculate something across related rows without collapsing them like GROUP BY does. For example, a running total or each employee's salary rank within their department, using OVER (PARTITION BY department ORDER BY salary DESC). With ties: ROW_NUMBER gives unique numbers 1, 2, 3 even for ties, so add a tie breaker column to make it deterministic. RANK gives ties the same number and then skips, like 1, 1, 3. DENSE_RANK gives ties the same number without gaps, like 1, 1, 2. LAG and LEAD let you compare a row with the previous or next one.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Compute over a window of rows, keeping every row
    • PARTITION BY groups, ORDER BY sets order
    • ROW_NUMBER vs RANK vs DENSE_RANK on ties
    • Uses: top N per group, running totals, LAG and LEAD for changes

    What they're really checking: Window functions are the core of most data SQL rounds in 2026.

    📖 Learn it: SQL basics: asking a database questions · SQL JOINs: combining tables

  16. 20Write a query to get the top 3 products by revenue in each category.

    💬 Easy answer

    I'd rank products within each category and then filter. First, in a subquery or CTE, compute revenue per product and add DENSE_RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY category ORDER BY revenue DESC) AS rnk. Then select rows WHERE rnk <= 3. I'd say out loud how I handle ties: DENSE_RANK keeps all tied products, which could return more than 3 rows; if they want exactly 3, I'd use ROW_NUMBER with a tie breaker like product ID.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • CTE to aggregate revenue per product
    • Window rank partitioned by category
    • Filter rank ≤ 3 in the outer query (you can't filter a window function in WHERE directly)
    • State how ties are handled

    What they're really checking: A classic SQL task. Explaining tie handling shows maturity.

    📖 Learn it: SQL basics: asking a database questions

  17. 21What's the difference between correlation and causation? How would you prove causation?

    💬 Easy answer

    Correlation means two things move together; causation means one actually causes the other. Ice cream sales and drownings are correlated because both rise in summer, not because ice cream causes drowning. In products, users who use a new feature might retain better simply because they were already more engaged. To show causation, the best tool is a randomised A/B test: randomly give the feature to half the users, so the only systematic difference is the feature, then compare outcomes.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Correlation ≠ causation; hidden third factors (confounders)
    • Selection bias: who opts in is different
    • Randomised experiment is the gold standard

    What they're really checking: Statistical thinking for decisions; critical for PMs and analysts.

    📖 Learn it: Correlation vs causation, and sampling bias · Metrics and A/B testing for AI features

🛠️ Role skills

The technical heart of the interview for this role. Interviewers go deeper with follow up questions, so understand the why, not just the definition.

  1. 22How do you approach feature engineering for a new problem?

    💬 Easy answer

    I start from the domain: what would a human expert look at to make this decision? For predicting late loan payments, that's things like income to EMI ratio, past late payments and recent changes in spending. Then I clean and encode: handle missing values, one hot encode categories, scale numbers if the model needs it, and create time based features like "days since last purchase". I check every feature for leakage and measure value with validation scores and feature importance. Usually a few good domain features beat many random ones.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Start with domain knowledge and the decision being made
    • Clean, encode, scale, create ratios and time features
    • Check each feature for leakage
    • Validate importance with experiments

    What they're really checking: Practical modelling sense. Domain driven features impress more than tool lists.

    📖 Learn it: Feature engineering: turning raw data into useful inputs

  2. 23How do you handle an imbalanced dataset, like 1% fraud?

    💬 Easy answer

    First, choose the right metric: precision, recall, PR AUC, not accuracy. Then options: use class weights so mistakes on the rare class cost more; resample, either undersample the majority or oversample the minority (SMOTE), only on the training data; and tune the decision threshold to hit the recall or precision the business needs. Also make sure the validation split keeps the same ratio, using a stratified split. Often, getting more examples of the rare class or better features helps most.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Right metric first
    • Class weights, resampling (train only), threshold tuning
    • Stratified splits
    • More data or better features for the rare class

    What they're really checking: A follow up to the accuracy question; very common.

    📖 Learn it: Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall

  3. 24How do you deal with missing values?

    💬 Easy answer

    First I ask why they're missing, because that changes the fix. If income is missing because people didn't want to share it, the fact that it's missing may itself be a signal. Options: drop rows if very few are missing; fill with the median or most common value; fill using a model or a group average; or add a "was missing" flag column alongside the filled value. Tree models like XGBoost can handle missing values natively. Whatever I do, I fit the filling logic on the training data only.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Understand why it's missing first
    • Drop, impute (median, mode, model), or add a missing flag
    • Fit imputation on train only to avoid leakage

    What they're really checking: Data cleaning judgement, not just a pandas command.

    📖 Learn it: Pandas: working with tables of data · Feature engineering: turning raw data into useful inputs · Data quality: catching bad data early

  4. 25How do you choose which model to use?

    💬 Easy answer

    I start with a simple baseline, like logistic regression or even a rule, so I know what "good" means. For tabular data, I'd then try gradient boosting because it's usually strongest. For images, text or audio, I'd use pretrained deep learning models or an LLM. Then I consider constraints: latency, cost, how much data I have, and whether the result must be explainable, for example in lending. I pick the simplest model that meets the target on validation data, because it's easier to maintain.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Start with a baseline
    • Data type guides the family (tables → boosting; text/images → pretrained deep models)
    • Constraints: latency, interpretability, data size, cost
    • Simplest model that meets the goal

    What they're really checking: Engineering judgement over chasing the fanciest model.

    📖 Learn it: Two kinds of prediction: regression and classification · Decision trees, random forests and boosting

  5. 26What steps do you follow in a machine learning project, from start to finish?

    💬 Easy answer

    One, understand the problem and the success metric with the people who need it. Two, collect the data and explore it: size, missing values, distributions and obvious errors. Three, clean the data and create features. Four, split into training, validation and test sets. Five, train a simple baseline, then better models, comparing them on validation data. Six, evaluate the final model once on the test set and check its mistakes. Seven, deploy it, for example as an API or a Streamlit app. Eight, monitor it and retrain when data changes.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Problem and metric first
    • Data exploration and cleaning
    • Baseline, then improvement on validation
    • Test once, deploy, monitor

    What they're really checking: Do you see the whole life cycle, not just model.fit()?

    📖 Learn it: How machines learn: features, labels and training · Train/test split and overfitting · Training vs inference: how ML runs in production

  6. 27You get a new dataset. How do you explore it?

    💬 Easy answer

    I start with the shape and column types, then look at the first few rows to understand what each column means. Next, missing values per column, duplicates, and basic statistics like mean, median, minimum and maximum, which reveal impossible values like negative ages. I plot distributions with histograms and count the categories. Then I look at relationships: how each feature relates to the target, and correlations between features. I write down surprises and questions for the data owner, because many problems are understood only by asking.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Shape, types, sample rows
    • Missing values, duplicates, summary stats, outliers
    • Distributions and relationships with the target
    • Note questions for the data owner

    What they're really checking: Practical data sense; freshers are often asked to describe this step by step.

    📖 Learn it: Pandas: working with tables of data · Averages and spread: mean, median, variance

  7. 28How did you learn AI, and what are you learning right now?

    💬 Easy answer

    Be concrete and honest. "I started with Python and statistics, then did a structured machine learning course and built small projects on Kaggle datasets. The biggest jump came from building end to end projects: a loan default model I deployed on Streamlit, and a PDF question answering app with an LLM API. Right now I'm learning how to evaluate LLM apps properly, because my first version gave wrong answers I didn't notice until I built a test set."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • A clear learning path
    • Projects, not just certificates
    • What you're learning now and why
    • Honest about what you don't know yet

    What they're really checking: Learning ability matters most for freshers.

    📖 Learn it: Building a portfolio project that gets interviews · Landing the AI role: resume, applications and interviews

🏗️ Design and case questions

Open questions with no single right answer. Clarify first, then structure your answer, then talk about trade offs, measurement and failure cases.

  1. 29How would you build a spam email classifier?

    💬 Easy answer

    Clarify: what counts as spam, and which mistake is worse? Usually marking a real email as spam is worse, so precision matters. Data: emails labelled spam or not. Features: start with text turned into numbers, using TF IDF, plus simple signals like number of links or the sender's history. Model: a baseline with logistic regression or naive Bayes, which works well for text, then compare with a fine tuned small language model if needed. Evaluate with precision and recall on a test set, and pick a threshold that keeps false positives very low. Deploy it as a service, collect "not spam" corrections from users, and retrain regularly because spammers change tactics.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Clarify which mistake is worse
    • Text features (TF IDF) plus extra signals
    • Simple baseline first
    • Precision focused threshold; feedback and retraining

    What they're really checking: A classic fresher design question: a full, simple pipeline with good reasoning.

    📖 Learn it: Two kinds of prediction: regression and classification · Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall · Feature engineering: turning raw data into useful inputs

  2. 30How would you build a chatbot that answers students' questions from college notices and PDFs?

    💬 Easy answer

    I'd use RAG. First, collect the notices and PDFs, extract their text, split it into chunks of a few paragraphs, create embeddings and store them in a vector database. When a student asks a question, embed the question, find the most similar chunks, and send them to an LLM with the instruction to answer only from them and to show which notice the answer came from. If nothing relevant is found, it should say so and point to the office. I'd test it with 30 real student questions, fix the failures, deploy it with Streamlit, and add a feedback button.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • RAG: ingest, chunk, embed, store
    • Retrieve and answer with citations
    • "I don't know" when there's no answer
    • Test set, deployment, feedback

    What they're really checking: Can a fresher design a realistic GenAI project end to end?

    📖 Learn it: RAG: letting an LLM answer from your documents · Embeddings and vector databases: searching by meaning · Evaluating AI: how to know it actually works

🤝 Projects and behaviour

Use the STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Include a number in the result whenever you can.

  1. 31Walk me through a project you're proud of.

    💬 Easy answer

    Use STAR and go deep on your own decisions. "Situation: our support team was overwhelmed with repeated questions. Task: I wanted to build an assistant that answers from our help docs. Action: I built a RAG pipeline, but the first version answered only 60% of test questions correctly. I created an evaluation set of 80 real questions, found that most failures came from chunks splitting tables, switched to structure aware chunking and added reranking. Python & coding

  1. What does len({'a': 1, 'b': 2, 'a': 3}) return?

    1. It raises an error
    2. 2
    3. 3
    4. 1
    Show answer

    2. Dictionary keys are unique. The second 'a' overwrites the first, leaving 2 keys.

    Learn: Lists and dictionaries: storing many values

  2. You need to check whether 1 million IDs are in a collection of 1 million known IDs. What's the efficient approach?

    1. Keep the known IDs in a list and use `in`
    2. Sort both lists and compare index by index in a nested loop
    3. Convert the IDs to strings first
    4. Put the known IDs in a set and use `in`
    Show answer

    Put the known IDs in a set and use `in`. Membership checks in a set are O(1) on average, but O(n) in a list. With a list this becomes about a trillion comparisons.

    Learn: Lists and dictionaries: storing many values

  3. What's wrong with `def add(item, basket=[]): basket.append(item); return basket`?

    1. Nothing, it's idiomatic
    2. The default list is created once and shared between calls, so items leak across calls
    3. append returns None, so the function always returns None
    4. Lists can't be default arguments
    Show answer

    The default list is created once and shared between calls, so items leak across calls. Default values are evaluated once, when the function is defined. Use `basket=None` and create a new list inside the function.

    Learn: Python logic: if/else, loops and functions

  4. What does df.groupby('city')['amount'].mean() return?

    1. The overall mean amount
    2. The cities sorted by amount
    3. One average amount per city
    4. A count of rows per city
    Show answer

    One average amount per city. groupby splits the rows by city, then mean() averages 'amount' within each group, giving a Series indexed by city.

    Learn: Pandas: working with tables of data

  1. Salaries in a team are mostly around ₹8 LPA, with one executive at ₹2 crore. Which best describes a typical salary?

    1. The median
    2. The mean
    3. The sum divided by 2
    4. The maximum
    Show answer

    The median. One extreme value pulls the mean up a lot. The median, the middle value, isn't affected by outliers.

    2. No. People who opt in may already be more engaged, so run a randomised A/B test
    3. No, time-in-app can never be measured
    4. Yes, the difference is large
    Show answer

    No. People who opt in may already be more engaged, so run a randomised A/B test. This is self-selection: the opt-in group differs from everyone else. Only random assignment isolates the feature's effect.

    2. ORDER BY COUNT(*) > 100
    3. LIMIT 100

    Show answer

    Show answer

ML fundamentals

  1. Training accuracy is 98% and test accuracy is 65%. What's happening, and what's a reasonable fix?

    1. Underfitting, so add more layers
    2. Everything is fine
    3. Overfitting, so simplify the model, regularise or get more data
    4. The test set is wrong, so delete it
    Show answer

    Overfitting, so simplify the model, regularise or get more data. A large train/test gap means the model memorised the training data. Reduce its complexity, regularise, or add data.

    Learn: Train/test split and overfitting

  2. A fraud model is 99% accurate, and 1% of transactions are fraud. What's your first reaction?

    1. Increase the epochs
    2. Accuracy is the only metric that matters
    3. Great, ship it
    4. Compare against the baseline and check precision and recall, because predicting 'not fraud' for everything also scores 99%
    Show answer

    Compare against the baseline and check precision and recall, because predicting 'not fraud' for everything also scores 99%. With rare classes, accuracy is misleading. Precision and recall show whether fraud is actually being caught.

    Learn: Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall

  3. A fraud model uses 'refund_issued' as a feature and scores 0.99 AUC in testing. What's the problem?

    1. Data leakage: refunds happen after fraud is confirmed, so this information isn't available at prediction time
    2. The feature needs scaling
    3. AUC can't exceed 0.95
    4. Nothing, it's a strong feature
    Show answer

    Data leakage: refunds happen after fraud is confirmed, so this information isn't available at prediction time. Features must only use information available at the moment of prediction. Otherwise test scores are inflated.

    Learn: Feature engineering: turning raw data into useful inputs

  4. For a churn model on a 30-column customer table, what's a strong first choice after a baseline?

    1. A chatbot
    2. Gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost or LightGBM
    3. A large transformer
    4. K-means clustering
    Show answer

    Gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost or LightGBM. Boosted trees are usually the most accurate, fastest and most explainable option for tabular data.

  5. For a cancer-screening model, which metric should you usually prioritise?

    1. Model size
    2. Recall
    3. Training speed
    4. Precision
    Show answer

    Recall. Missing a real case is far more costly than a false alarm, which only leads to another test.

  6. Explain overfitting to a non-technical person, and how you'd detect and fix it. open question

    A strong answer covers:

    • A simple analogy (memorising past papers)
    • Detection: a gap between training and test scores
    • Cross-validation for a reliable estimate
    • Fixes: a simpler model, regularisation, more data, early stopping
    • A small numeric example
    Show model answer

    Overfitting is like a student who memorises last year's paper word for word and then struggles with new questions. To detect it, I keep a test set the model never sees: if training accuracy is 98% but test accuracy is 70%, it has memorised rather than learned. Cross-validation gives a more reliable estimate. To fix it, I'd use a simpler model or regularisation, get more or cleaner data, or stop training earlier.

LLMs & prompting

  1. You need consistent, repeatable answers for extracting invoice fields. How should you set temperature?

    1. High (1.0+)
    2. Change it randomly per request
    3. Low (around 0)
    4. It doesn't matter
    Show answer

    Low (around 0). Low temperature makes outputs focused and repeatable. Higher temperature adds variety, which suits brainstorming.

  2. Why do LLMs sometimes state wrong facts confidently?

    1. Only small models do this
    2. Their internet connection is slow
    3. They are lying on purpose
    4. They generate plausible text and don't verify facts unless given sources or tools
    Show answer

    They generate plausible text and don't verify facts unless given sources or tools. Grounding answers in sources (RAG), using tools, and showing citations reduces hallucination.

  3. In simple words, what is an LLM, and what is one important limitation? open question

    A strong answer covers:

    • It predicts the next token, trained on huge amounts of text
    • Tokens and the context window
    • It generates plausible text, not verified facts
    • The hallucination limitation, with an example
    • A mitigation: RAG, tools, citations
    Show model answer

    An LLM is a model trained on a huge amount of text to predict the next word, or token. Doing that at scale lets it write, summarise and answer questions. It works within a context window, a limit on how much text it can consider at once. A key limitation is that it produces plausible text rather than checked facts, so it can confidently state something wrong. You reduce this by giving it trusted sources at question time (RAG), asking for citations, and using tools for calculations or live data.

ML system design

  1. Design a fraud-detection system for UPI payments. open question

    A strong answer covers:

    • Requirements: latency budget (e.g. under 100 ms), volume, the cost of a miss vs a false alarm
    • Features: real-time (amount vs the user's usual, velocity) and historical, with no leakage
    • Model choice (e.g. gradient boosting) and threshold set by business cost
    • Serving: a low-latency API plus a streaming feature store
    • Monitoring: drift, delayed fraud labels as feedback, and retraining
    • A human review queue for borderline cases
    Show model answer

    I'd start from requirements: decisions in under 100 ms at peak volume, and an explicit cost for missed fraud vs blocking a genuine payment. Features combine real-time signals, such as amount versus the user's usual, transactions in the last 10 minutes and a new device or payee, with historical aggregates from a feature store, all computed only from data available at payment time. A gradient-boosted model fits tabular signals well. I'd set the threshold to minimise expected cost and send a middle band to manual review. It would be served behind a low-latency API with streaming feature updates. I'd monitor input drift and precision and recall as chargeback labels arrive weeks later, and retrain on a schedule.
