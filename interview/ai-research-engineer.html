👋 Opening: about you

Almost every interview starts here. These answers set the tone, so prepare them word for word and practise them out loud.

  1. 1Tell me about yourself.

    💬 Easy answer

    Use present, past, future, in about 60 to 90 seconds. Present: "I'm a Java backend developer with four years at a fintech company, where I build payment APIs." Past: "Over the last year I moved towards AI. I built a document question answering app with RAG and an evaluation set, and I used an LLM to classify support tickets at work, which cut manual triage by about 30%." Future: "Now I want to do this full time as an AI engineer, and this role fits because you build LLM features on top of real backend systems, which is exactly where my strengths meet."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Present: current role and one concrete thing you do
    • Past: the 1 or 2 experiences that lead to this role, with a number
    • Future: why this role and this company is the logical next step
    • Keep it under 2 minutes; don't recite your whole resume

    What they're really checking: Can you tell a clear, relevant story about yourself? It's also a test of communication: structure and confidence matter more than detail.

  2. 2Why do you want to move into AI, and why this particular role?

    💬 Easy answer

    Give a real reason and connect it to evidence. "In my testing work I kept seeing the same problem: thousands of test cases written and checked by hand. When I tried an LLM to draft test cases, it saved hours, but it also made mistakes I had to catch. That got me interested in building AI systems that are actually reliable. I've since completed projects in RAG and evaluation. I'm applying for this role because it focuses on shipping AI features to users, and my background in quality and testing directly helps with evaluating AI output."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • A specific moment or problem that sparked the interest (not "AI is the future")
    • Proof you've acted on it: courses finished, projects built
    • How your previous experience transfers to this role
    • Why this role, not just AI in general

    What they're really checking: Is your interest genuine and backed by action? Interviewers worry about people chasing hype who will lose interest.

    💬 Easy answer

I like that your team publishes how you evaluate models, because I care about measuring AI quality. And since you serve small businesses in India, my experience building for that market would help from day one."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Something specific about their product or users
    • Something about their team, values or engineering approach
    • Why you fit: a direct link to your skills

    What they're really checking: Have you researched the company, and are you likely to stay?

🧱 Fundamentals

Short concept questions to check your basics. Answer in 3 to 5 sentences, with one example.

  1. 4Explain supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning, with an example of each.

    💬 Easy answer

    Supervised learning learns from examples that have the right answer, called labels. For example, emails marked spam or not spam, so the model learns to predict spam. Unsupervised learning has no labels; it finds structure by itself, like grouping customers into segments by behaviour. Reinforcement learning learns by trial and error with rewards, like a game playing agent or a robot that gets a reward when it does the task well. LLMs also use reinforcement learning from human feedback to make answers more helpful.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Supervised = labelled data, predicts a known target
    • Unsupervised = no labels, finds groups or patterns
    • Reinforcement = actions, rewards, learning by trial and error
    • A real example for each

    What they're really checking: Basic vocabulary and whether you can give examples on the spot.

    📖 Learn it: How machines learn: features, labels and training · Two kinds of prediction: regression and classification

  2. 5What is overfitting, and how do you prevent it?

    💬 Easy answer

    Overfitting is when a model memorises the training data, including its noise, so it scores very well on training data but badly on new data. It's like a student who memorises last year's answers but can't solve a new question. You spot it when training accuracy is high and validation accuracy is much lower. To prevent it: get more data, use a simpler model, add regularisation, use cross validation, stop training early, and for neural networks use dropout.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Definition: good on training data, poor on unseen data
    • How to detect: compare train vs validation scores
    • At least three fixes: more data, simpler model, regularisation, early stopping, dropout
    • Mention underfitting as the opposite

    What they're really checking: The single most asked ML concept. They want the detection method, not only the definition.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  3. 6What is the bias variance trade off?

    💬 Easy answer

    Bias is error from a model that's too simple, so it misses real patterns. That's underfitting. Variance is error from a model that's too sensitive to the training data, so small changes in the data change it a lot. That's overfitting. Making a model more complex usually lowers bias but raises variance. The goal is the sweet spot where total error on new data is lowest, which we find using validation data. For example, a straight line through curved data has high bias; a very deep decision tree has high variance.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Bias = too simple, underfits
    • Variance = too sensitive, overfits
    • Complexity trades one for the other; pick by validation error
    • An example of each

    What they're really checking: Whether you understand why models fail, not just how to train them.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  4. 7Why do we split data into training, validation and test sets?

    💬 Easy answer

    The training set is what the model learns from. The validation set is used while building, to compare models and tune settings like depth or learning rate. The test set is kept locked away until the very end to get an honest estimate of how the model will do on new data. If you tune on the test set, you slowly overfit to it and your final number becomes too optimistic. For time based data, split by time, so you train on the past and test on the future.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Train = learn, validation = choose and tune, test = final honest check
    • Never tune on the test set
    • Split by time for time series; split by user or group to avoid leakage

    What they're really checking: Experiment discipline. Many candidates forget the role of the validation set.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  5. 8What is data leakage? Give an example.

    💬 Easy answer

    Leakage is when information that won't be available at prediction time sneaks into training, so the model looks great in testing and then fails in production. A classic example: predicting whether a customer will cancel, and including a feature like "refund issued", which only happens after they cancel. Another is scaling features using statistics from the whole dataset before splitting, so the test set influences training. To avoid it, ask for each feature: would I know this value at the moment I make the prediction? And do all preprocessing inside the training split.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Definition: future or target information in the training features
    • A concrete example (post outcome feature, preprocessing before split, duplicate rows across splits)
    • Symptom: suspiciously high scores
    • Prevention: point in time features, pipelines fitted on train only

    What they're really checking: A favourite for experienced ML candidates, because it shows real world scars.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting · Feature engineering: turning raw data into useful inputs

  6. 9Explain precision and recall. When would you prefer one over the other?

    💬 Easy answer

    Precision: of everything the model flagged as positive, how many were really positive. Recall: of all the real positives, how many the model caught. For a cancer screening test or fraud detection, missing a real case is very costly, so we prefer high recall, even if we get some false alarms. For a spam filter, putting a real email into spam is annoying, so we prefer high precision. F1 score combines both when you need one number, and you can move between them by changing the decision threshold.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Precision = correctness of positive predictions
    • Recall = coverage of actual positives
    • Choose based on which mistake costs more, with an example
    • Threshold controls the trade off; F1 balances them

    What they're really checking: Whether you can connect a metric to a business cost.

    📖 Learn it: Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall

  7. 10Your model is 99% accurate, but the business says it's useless. How is that possible?

    💬 Easy answer

    Probably the classes are imbalanced. If only 1% of transactions are fraud, a model that always says "not fraud" is 99% accurate and catches zero fraud. Accuracy hides that. I'd look at the confusion matrix, precision and recall for the rare class, and metrics like PR AUC. Then I'd check the business goal: maybe they need to catch 80% of fraud while keeping false alarms low enough for the review team to handle, and I'd tune the threshold for that.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Class imbalance makes accuracy misleading
    • Use confusion matrix, precision, recall, PR AUC
    • Tie the metric to the business goal and capacity
    • Fixes: threshold tuning, class weights, resampling

    What they're really checking: Can you think beyond the headline number? Very common in 2026 interviews.

    📖 Learn it: Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall

  8. 11What is regularisation? What's the difference between L1 and L2?

    💬 Easy answer

    Regularisation adds a penalty for large weights to the loss, which keeps the model simpler and reduces overfitting. L2, also called ridge, penalises the square of weights, so it shrinks all weights smoothly towards zero but rarely to exactly zero. L1, also called lasso, penalises the absolute value, which pushes some weights to exactly zero, so it also does feature selection. In neural networks we also use dropout and weight decay, which is L2 in practice.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Penalty on weight size added to the loss
    • L2 shrinks smoothly; L1 makes some weights exactly zero
    • Strength is a hyperparameter tuned on validation data

    What they're really checking: Standard ML theory; they may follow up with why L1 creates zeros.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting · Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve

  9. 12Explain gradient descent in simple words. What does the learning rate do?

    💬 Easy answer

    Imagine you're on a hill in fog and want to reach the bottom. You feel which way the ground slopes and take a step downhill, again and again. Gradient descent does the same with the model's error: the gradient tells us which direction increases the error, so we move the weights a small step in the opposite direction. The learning rate is the step size. Too big and you overshoot and bounce around; too small and training is very slow. In practice we use mini batches and optimisers like Adam.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Loss = how wrong the model is; gradient = direction of steepest increase
    • Update weights opposite to the gradient
    • Learning rate = step size, with the risk of too big or too small
    • Mention mini batch SGD or Adam

    What they're really checking: Do you understand how models actually learn?

    📖 Learn it: Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve

🛠️ Role skills

The technical heart of the interview for this role. Interviewers go deeper with follow up questions, so understand the why, not just the definition.

  1. 13What is cross validation and when would you not use standard k fold?

    💬 Easy answer

    K fold cross validation splits training data into k parts, trains on k minus 1 and validates on the remaining part, rotating through all parts. Averaging gives a more reliable estimate than a single split, which matters with small data. I wouldn't use plain k fold for time series, because it lets the model see the future; I'd use time based splits instead. And if one user has many rows, I'd use group k fold so the same user isn't in both training and validation.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Rotate validation folds; average the scores
    • Useful for small datasets and tuning
    • Time series → forward chaining splits
    • Grouped data → group k fold

    What they're really checking: Knowing when the standard tool breaks.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  2. 14How would you implement logistic regression from scratch?

    💬 Easy answer

    I'd explain the steps, then code them in NumPy. One, initialise weights and bias to zeros. Two, compute z = X·w + b and the prediction p = sigmoid(z). Three, the loss is binary cross entropy. Four, the gradient is simple: dw = Xᵀ(p − y) / n and db = mean(p − y). Five, update w and b by subtracting learning rate times the gradient, and loop for some epochs. I'd add a check that the loss goes down, clip probabilities to avoid log of zero, and test on a tiny dataset where I know the answer.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Sigmoid of a linear combination
    • Binary cross entropy loss
    • Gradient = Xᵀ(p − y)/n
    • Update loop; sanity checks for numerical stability

    What they're really checking: ML coding rounds in 2026 often ask for algorithms from scratch, not just library calls.

    📖 Learn it: Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve · Two kinds of prediction: regression and classification

  3. 15Explain self attention. Why do we divide by the square root of d_k?

    💬 Easy answer

    Each token creates three vectors: a query, "what am I looking for?", a key, "what do I contain?", and a value, "what do I pass on?". For each token, we compare its query with every key using a dot product, which gives relevance scores. Softmax turns the scores into weights, and the output is a weighted sum of the values. So each token builds a new representation by looking at the others. We divide by the square root of d_k because dot products grow with dimension; without scaling, softmax becomes very peaked and its gradients become tiny, which slows training.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Q, K, V vectors from each token
    • Scores = Q·Kᵀ, softmax, weighted sum of V
    • Scaling keeps softmax from saturating
    • Multi head = several attentions in parallel learning different relations

    What they're really checking: The classic research and ML question; they want the intuition and the math.

    📖 Learn it: Transformers and attention: how LLMs read

  4. 16What's the computational cost of self attention, and how can it be reduced?

    💬 Easy answer

    For a sequence of n tokens with dimension d, attention compares every token with every other token, so time is roughly O(n² · d) and the attention matrix needs O(n²) memory. That's why long contexts are expensive. Approaches to reduce it include FlashAttention, which computes exact attention in tiles to save memory and is much faster on GPUs; sparse or sliding window attention, which only looks at nearby tokens; linear attention approximations; and at inference, the KV cache plus grouped query attention to reduce memory per token.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • O(n²) in sequence length
    • FlashAttention: exact, memory efficient, IO aware
    • Sparse or windowed attention, linear approximations
    • Inference tricks: KV cache, GQA

    What they're really checking: Depth on efficiency, which labs care about.

    📖 Learn it: Transformers and attention: how LLMs read · Serving LLMs efficiently: GPUs, batching and cost

  5. 17What is the KV cache in LLM inference?

    💬 Easy answer

    When an LLM generates text one token at a time, each new token needs attention over all previous tokens. Without a cache, we'd recompute the keys and values of every previous token at every step, which is wasteful. The KV cache stores the keys and values computed so far, so each step only computes them for the new token. That makes generation much faster, but the cache uses a lot of GPU memory, growing with sequence length and batch size, which is why techniques like grouped query attention and paged attention, as in vLLM, matter.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Stores past keys and values during generation
    • Avoids recomputation; big speedup
    • Costs GPU memory; grows with length and batch
    • GQA and paged attention manage that memory

    What they're really checking: Inference fundamentals, asked by labs and LLM infra teams.

    📖 Learn it: Transformers and attention: how LLMs read · Serving LLMs efficiently: GPUs, batching and cost

  6. 18What's the gradient of softmax combined with cross entropy loss?

    💬 Easy answer

    If p is the softmax output and y is the one hot true label, the gradient of the cross entropy loss with respect to the logits is simply p − y. Intuitively, for the correct class the gradient is p − 1, which is negative, so training pushes that logit up; for wrong classes it's p, pushing those logits down in proportion to how confident the model wrongly was. This clean result is why softmax and cross entropy are combined in one function in frameworks, which is also more numerically stable using the log sum exp trick.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Gradient w.r.t. logits = p − y
    • Intuition for correct vs wrong classes
    • Implemented together for numerical stability (log sum exp)

    What they're really checking: Labs report this as a quick filter: can you derive basics cleanly?

    📖 Learn it: Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve · Neural networks: layers that learn

  7. 19Walk me through implementing multi head attention with a causal mask.

    💬 Easy answer

    Input x has shape batch × sequence × d_model. One, project x with three linear layers into Q, K and V. Two, reshape each to batch × heads × sequence × head_dim, where head_dim = d_model / heads. Three, scores = Q·Kᵀ / √head_dim, giving batch × heads × seq × seq. Four, for a causal mask, set scores above the diagonal to minus infinity, so a token can't see the future. Five, softmax over the last dimension, optional dropout, then multiply by V. Six, merge the heads back to batch × sequence × d_model and apply an output projection. I'd test shapes at each step and check that changing a future token doesn't change earlier outputs.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Q, K, V projections and reshape into heads
    • Scaled scores, causal mask with −inf above the diagonal
    • Softmax, weighted sum, merge heads, output projection
    • Tests: shapes and no leakage from the future

    What they're really checking: A live coding baseline at many labs in 2026.

    📖 Learn it: Transformers and attention: how LLMs read

  8. 20What is LoRA, and why is it popular?

    💬 Easy answer

    LoRA, low rank adaptation, fine tunes a big model by freezing the original weights and learning a small update for some weight matrices, written as the product of two thin matrices, A and B. Because the rank is small, you train maybe less than 1% of the parameters, which needs far less GPU memory and storage. You can keep one base model and swap small LoRA adapters for different tasks. QLoRA goes further by keeping the base model in 4 bit precision, so you can fine tune large models on a single GPU.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Freeze base weights; learn low rank update A·B
    • Far fewer trainable parameters and memory
    • Swappable adapters per task
    • QLoRA: quantised base for even less memory

    What they're really checking: Practical fine tuning knowledge.

    📖 Learn it: Fine-tuning and LoRA: specialising a model

  9. 21What are vanishing gradients, and what fixed them in modern networks?

    💬 Easy answer

    In deep networks, gradients are multiplied through many layers during backpropagation. If those factors are below one, the gradient shrinks to nearly zero by the time it reaches early layers, so they stop learning. Fixes that made deep networks work: activations like ReLU instead of sigmoid; residual or skip connections, which give gradients a direct path back; normalisation layers like LayerNorm; careful weight initialisation; and optimisers like Adam. Transformers rely heavily on residual connections and LayerNorm.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Gradients shrink through many layers
    • ReLU, residual connections, normalisation, good initialisation
    • Link to transformers' design

    What they're really checking: Deep learning fundamentals.

    📖 Learn it: Neural networks: layers that learn · Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve

  10. 22How do you reproduce a paper, and what do you do when your results don't match?

    💬 Easy answer

    I read the paper for its core claim and the exact setup: data, preprocessing, model size, hyperparameters and evaluation. If code exists, I run it first to get a reference. Then I implement step by step, testing each part on tiny data, and match the simplest experiment before the full one. If results don't match, I check the usual suspects: evaluation differences, data preprocessing, random seeds and variance across runs, learning rate schedule, and details hidden in the appendix. I log every experiment, and if a gap remains, I write it up honestly; that's useful research too.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Identify the claim and exact setup
    • Start from tiny, verifiable steps
    • Debug the usual suspects; run several seeds
    • Track experiments and report gaps honestly

    What they're really checking: Research discipline and honesty.

    📖 Learn it: Reading and reproducing research papers

  11. 23What is an ablation study and why does it matter?

    💬 Easy answer

    An ablation study removes or changes one part of a system at a time to see how much each part contributes. If my new model has three changes and improves accuracy by 4 points, ablations tell me whether all three matter or one change does all the work. It matters because it turns "it works" into "we know why it works", prevents unnecessary complexity, and is expected in any credible paper. Good ablations change one thing at a time and use several random seeds, so differences aren't just noise.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Remove one component at a time
    • Attribute gains to specific changes
    • One change at a time, multiple seeds

    What they're really checking: Experimental design thinking.

    📖 Learn it: Reading and reproducing research papers

  12. 24How do you make model training reproducible?

    💬 Easy answer

    Version everything that affects the result: the code with git, the data with a snapshot or tool like DVC, the environment with a Docker image and pinned packages, and the configuration and hyperparameters in a tracked config file. Set random seeds. Log every run, with its code commit, data version, parameters, metrics and the resulting model, in an experiment tracker like MLflow. Then anyone can rerun or explain a model months later, which also matters for audits.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Version code, data, environment and config
    • Seeds
    • Experiment tracking linking all of it to the model

    What they're really checking: Engineering discipline.

    📖 Learn it: MLOps: experiment tracking and model registries · Git and GitHub: saving and sharing your work

  13. 25How do you serve an open source LLM efficiently on GPUs?

    💬 Easy answer

    Use an inference server built for LLMs, like vLLM or TGI, rather than a plain web server. They provide continuous batching, which adds new requests into the running batch so the GPU stays busy, and paged attention to manage KV cache memory. Choose the smallest model that meets quality, and consider quantisation, like 8 bit or 4 bit, to fit on cheaper GPUs. Then autoscale on queue length or tokens per second, set max token limits, and monitor time to first token, tokens per second, GPU memory and cost per request.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • LLM inference server (vLLM, TGI)
    • Continuous batching, paged KV cache
    • Right sized and quantised models
    • Autoscale and monitor latency, throughput, cost

    What they're really checking: LLM infrastructure is now part of most MLOps interviews.

    📖 Learn it: Serving LLMs efficiently: GPUs, batching and cost

🏗️ Design and case questions

Open questions with no single right answer. Clarify first, then structure your answer, then talk about trade offs, measurement and failure cases.

  1. 26Design a platform to serve an LLM to 1,000 requests per second.

    💬 Easy answer

    Clarify model size, prompt and output lengths, and latency targets, especially time to first token. Requests go through a gateway with authentication, rate limits per customer and routing. Behind it, a pool of GPU servers running an inference engine like vLLM with continuous batching and paged KV cache, autoscaled on queue depth. Add a prefix and response cache for repeated prompts, and route simple requests to a smaller model. Estimate capacity from tokens per second per GPU, plan headroom for peaks, and spread across zones. Monitor time to first token, tokens per second, queue time, errors and cost per thousand tokens, and roll out new model versions with canaries.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Clarify sizes and latency targets
    • Gateway: auth, rate limits, routing
    • Inference engine with batching, autoscaling on queue
    • Caching, model routing, capacity planning, monitoring, canaries

    What they're really checking: LLM infrastructure system design.

    📖 Learn it: Serving LLMs efficiently: GPUs, batching and cost · Kubernetes: running containers at scale

🤝 Projects and behaviour

Use the STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Include a number in the result whenever you can.

  1. 27Walk me through a project you're proud of.

    💬 Easy answer

    Use STAR and go deep on your own decisions. "Situation: our support team was overwhelmed with repeated questions. Task: I wanted to build an assistant that answers from our help docs. Action: I built a RAG pipeline, but the first version answered only 60% of test questions correctly. I created an evaluation set of 80 real questions, found that most failures came from chunks splitting tables, switched to structure aware chunking and added reranking. Result: correct answers rose to 86%, and in a pilot it resolved about a quarter of tickets without an agent." Be ready for "why did you choose that?" at every step.

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • STAR structure
    • Your personal decisions and trade offs
    • A problem you hit and how you solved it
    • A measurable result; what you'd do differently

    What they're really checking: Ownership, depth and honesty. Most interviews spend 10 to 20 minutes here.

    📖 Learn it: Building a portfolio project that gets interviews

  2. 28Tell me about a time something you built failed.

    💬 Easy answer

    Pick a real failure with a lesson, not a fake one. "I shipped a classification model that looked great offline, with 92% accuracy, but within two weeks users complained. I had split data randomly, and the same customers appeared in both training and test, so the score was too optimistic. I owned it with my manager, re split by customer, got a realistic 81%, and improved features to reach 86%. Since then, I always ask how the data is grouped before splitting, and I add a review of the evaluation setup to my checklist."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • A real failure where you were responsible
    • What you did immediately
    • The root cause, honestly
    • The lasting change in how you work

    What they're really checking: Ownership and learning. Blaming others is a red flag.

    📖 Learn it: Train/test split and overfitting

  3. 29Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate or stakeholder.

    💬 Easy answer

    Show you disagree with data and respect. "A product manager wanted to launch our AI reply feature to all customers. I was worried because our tests showed it sometimes made up refund policies. Instead of just saying no, I showed five real examples and the error rate from our eval set, and proposed launching to 10% of tickets with agent approval first. We agreed on criteria to expand. Two weeks later the error rate was below our threshold and we expanded. We kept a good relationship because we focused on the shared goal."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Situation and both perspectives fairly
    • You used evidence, not authority
    • A compromise or experiment
    • Outcome and the relationship afterwards

    What they're really checking: Collaboration and influence.

    📖 Learn it: Explaining AI to non-technical people

  4. 30Explain a technical concept to me as if I'm not technical.

    💬 Easy answer

    Pick something from your work and use an everyday analogy. For example, explaining RAG: "Imagine a very smart new employee who has read a lot but knows nothing about our company. If you ask them a policy question, they might confidently guess. RAG is like giving them the right pages of our policy manual before they answer, and asking them to point to the page. So answers are based on our actual documents, and you can check them." Then check understanding: "Does that make sense?"

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Everyday analogy
    • No jargon, or jargon explained
    • Why it matters to the listener
    • Check understanding

    What they're really checking: Communication, essential for FDE, PM and senior roles.

    📖 Learn it: Explaining AI to non-technical people

  5. 31How do you keep up with the fast changes in AI?

    💬 Easy answer

    Be specific and show you filter noise. "I follow a few high quality sources instead of everything: release notes from the main model providers, a couple of newsletters, and papers when something important comes up. But mostly I learn by building: when something new appears, like a new tool calling feature, I try it on a small project within a week and write a short note about what worked. I also review what changed in our own evals when we upgrade models, which teaches me more than reading headlines."

    ✅ A strong answer covers

    • Specific sources, not "everything"
    • Learning by building

Python & coding

  1. You need to check whether 1 million IDs are in a collection of 1 million known IDs. What's the efficient approach?

    1. Keep the known IDs in a list and use `in`
    2. Sort both lists and compare index by index in a nested loop
    3. Convert the IDs to strings first
    4. Put the known IDs in a set and use `in`
    Show answer

    Put the known IDs in a set and use `in`. Membership checks in a set are O(1) on average, but O(n) in a list. With a list this becomes about a trillion comparisons.

    Learn: Lists and dictionaries: storing many values

  2. What does df.groupby('city')['amount'].mean() return?

    1. The overall mean amount
    2. The cities sorted by amount
    3. One average amount per city
    4. A count of rows per city
    Show answer

    One average amount per city. groupby splits the rows by city, then mean() averages 'amount' within each group, giving a Series indexed by city.

Statistics & data

  1. Salaries in a team are mostly around ₹8 LPA, with one executive at ₹2 crore. Which best describes a typical salary?

    1. The median
    2. The mean
    3. The sum divided by 2
    4. The maximum
    Show answer

    The median. One extreme value pulls the mean up a lot. The median, the middle value, isn't affected by outliers.

    Learn: Averages and spread: mean, median, variance

  2. Users who turned on a new 'focus mode' spend 30% more time in the app. Can you say focus mode caused it?

    1. Yes, if the sample has more than 1,000 users
    2. No. People who opt in may already be more engaged, so run a randomised A/B test
    3. No, time-in-app can never be measured
    4. Yes, the difference is large
    Show answer

    No. People who opt in may already be more engaged, so run a randomised A/B test. This is self-selection: the opt-in group differs from everyone else. Only random assignment isolates the feature's effect.

  3. What does a 95% confidence interval for a model's accuracy mean?

    1. There's a 95% chance the model is correct on any given input
    2. 95% of the test set was used
    3. If we repeated the evaluation on many fresh samples, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true accuracy
    4. Accuracy is at least 95%
    Show answer

    If we repeated the evaluation on many fresh samples, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true accuracy. A confidence interval describes the procedure's long-run coverage of the true value, not a per-prediction probability.

ML fundamentals

  1. Training accuracy is 98% and test accuracy is 65%. What's happening, and what's a reasonable fix?

    1. Underfitting, so add more layers
    2. Everything is fine
    3. Overfitting, so simplify the model, regularise or get more data
    4. The test set is wrong, so delete it
    Show answer

    Overfitting, so simplify the model, regularise or get more data. A large train/test gap means the model memorised the training data. Reduce its complexity, regularise, or add data.

    Learn: Train/test split and overfitting

  2. A fraud model is 99% accurate, and 1% of transactions are fraud. What's your first reaction?

    1. Increase the epochs
    2. Accuracy is the only metric that matters
    3. Great, ship it
    4. Compare against the baseline and check precision and recall, because predicting 'not fraud' for everything also scores 99%
    Show answer

    Compare against the baseline and check precision and recall, because predicting 'not fraud' for everything also scores 99%. With rare classes, accuracy is misleading. Precision and recall show whether fraud is actually being caught.

    Learn: Measuring models: accuracy, precision and recall

  3. Why use k-fold cross-validation?

    1. It gives a more reliable performance estimate by averaging over several train/validation splits
    2. It replaces the need for a test set entirely
    3. It trains the final model faster
    4. It removes the need for features
    Show answer

    It gives a more reliable performance estimate by averaging over several train/validation splits. One split can be lucky or unlucky. Averaging across folds reduces that variance.

Deep learning

  1. Training loss jumps around wildly and never settles. What do you try first?

    1. Add more output classes
    2. Remove the validation set
    3. Increase the learning rate
    4. Decrease the learning rate
    Show answer

    Decrease the learning rate. Steps that are too large overshoot the minimum. A smaller learning rate, or a warm-up schedule, stabilises training.

    Learn: Gradients and gradient descent: how models improve

  2. What does self-attention compute in a transformer?

    1. The order in which to read files
    2. The learning rate per layer
    3. For each token, how much to draw on every other token, giving context-aware representations
    4. A compressed copy of the dataset
    Show answer

    For each token, how much to draw on every other token, giving context-aware representations. Queries and keys produce attention weights, and the values are mixed by those weights, so each token's meaning depends on its context.

    Learn: Transformers and attention: how LLMs read

  3. LoRA reduces fine-tuning cost mainly by…

    1. Training only the embeddings
    2. Using a smaller dataset
    3. Deleting half the layers
    4. Freezing the base weights and training small low-rank adapter matrices
    Show answer

    Freezing the base weights and training small low-rank adapter matrices. Only a tiny fraction of parameters are trained, which cuts memory and compute while keeping most of the quality.

    Learn: Fine-tuning and LoRA: specialising a model

  4. Why keep a validation set separate from the test set?

    1. To tune hyperparameters without biasing the final test estimate
    2. Because test sets are illegal to reuse
    3. To speed up inference
    4. To make the model bigger
    Show answer

    To tune hyperparameters without biasing the final test estimate. If you tune against the test set, it stops being an unbiased estimate of real-world performance.

    Learn: Train/test split and overfitting · Reading and reproducing research papers

  5. Vanishing gradients in deep networks are most associated with…

    1. Large batch sizes only
    2. Using too much data
    3. Using GPUs
    4. Saturating activations like sigmoid in many stacked layers, and are eased by ReLU, residual connections and normalisation
    Show answer

    Saturating activations like sigmoid in many stacked layers, and are eased by ReLU, residual connections and normalisation. Repeatedly multiplying small derivatives shrinks gradients towards zero. Modern architectures add shortcuts and normalisation to fix this.

    Learn: Neural networks: layers that learn

  6. Explain how a transformer works to an engineer, and how you'd reduce its training cost. open question

    A strong answer covers:

    • Tokens become embeddings, plus positional information
    • Self-attention: queries, keys and values, mixing information across tokens
    • Multi-head attention, feed-forward layers, residuals and normalisation
    • The next-token prediction objective
    • Cost levers: mixed precision, efficient attention, LoRA/PEFT, data quality, smaller models
    Show model answer

    Text is tokenised and each token mapped to a vector, with positional information added. In each layer, self-attention projects tokens into queries, keys and values; the attention weights from queries and keys mix the values, so each token's representation reflects its context. Multiple heads capture different relationships, followed by a feed-forward network, with residual connections and normalisation for stable training. The model is trained to predict the next token. To cut cost I'd use mixed precision, efficient attention kernels, a cleaner and deduplicated dataset, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning instead of full training where possible.

    Learn: Transformers and attention: how LLMs read · Fine-tuning and LoRA: specialising a model

ML system design

  1. Design a fraud-detection system for UPI payments. open question

    A strong answer covers:

    • Requirements: latency budget (e.g. under 100 ms), volume, the cost of a miss vs a false alarm
    • Features: real-time (amount vs the user's usual, velocity) and historical, with no leakage
    • Model choice (e.g. gradient boosting) and threshold set by business cost
    • Serving: a low-latency API plus a streaming feature store
    • Monitoring: drift, delayed fraud labels as feedback, and retraining
    • A human review queue for borderline cases
    Show model answer

    I'd start from requirements: decisions in under 100 ms at peak volume, and an explicit cost for missed fraud vs blocking a genuine payment. Features combine real-time signals, such as amount versus the user's usual, transactions in the last 10 minutes and a new device or payee, with historical aggregates from a feature store, all computed only from data available at payment time. A gradient-boosted model fits tabular signals well. I'd set the threshold to minimise expected cost and send a middle band to manual review. It would be served behind a low-latency API with streaming feature updates. I'd monitor input drift and precision and recall as chargeback labels arrive weeks later, and retrain on a schedule.
